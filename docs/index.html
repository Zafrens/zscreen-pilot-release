Teaching biology to read chemistry, one reusable part at a time

Z-Screen pairs combinatorial small molecules built from reusable building blocks with whole-transcriptome RNA and high-content imaging readouts in microfluidic nanowells. This portal presents the Z-Screen pilot dataset (162,914 compounds, 190,699 profiles, 11,435 same-well ActiveSeq wells) and what it shows: the measurements are learnable, they transfer to public data, and they match chemical and genetic reference responses.

Pillar 1 · Platform and combinatorial grammar

A reusable chemical language for learning cellular responses

A conventional screen tests a pile of unrelated molecules for a single yes/no signal. Z-Screen builds molecules like sentences from recurring chemical words, then reads out 6,000 genes per nanowell, with imaging paired on 33,575 compounds and measured inside the same well on 11,435 ActiveSeq wells.

Investor lens

The “Lego grammar” of molecules

In the pilot dataset, 162,914 molecules are assembled from 627 reusable building blocks in up to five positions. Each block appears in hundreds of molecules (median 461), and the dataset contains 5.08 million pairs of molecules that differ by a single piece.

  • Cells read individual parts. Swapping one piece predictably switches a cell from a heat-shock program to an ER-stress program.
  • Throughput. Up to 50,000 conditions per run at a company operating specification below $2 per condition, plus the ActiveSeq co-assay that reads microscopy and RNA from the exact same nanowell.
  • Scale. The pilot is a sample; the platform is designed for millions of compounds and beyond.
Technical lens

Compositional factorization and the ActiveSeq co-assay

Across 4 libraries (ZEL024, ZEL028, ZEL031, ZEL039) and 5 cell backgrounds (8 contexts), 46,944 raw genes are depth-normalized onto a 6,000-gene surface and projected onto a pinned k = 32 semi-NMF basis (W ∈ ℝ32×6000).

  • In silico building-block knockout. Removing a synthon from the trained model reproduces its empirical marginal effect at median Spearman r = 0.89–0.94 (z = 13.8–19.2 versus shuffled null); Morgan-substructure decomposition reconstructs whole-block effects at r = 0.97–0.99.
  • Chemistry-blind recurrence. Clustering 2,394 HEK293 compounds on RNA alone yields batch-recurrent directions (cosine advantage 0.581, p = 0.005) with 57 of 4,308 post-hoc synthon enrichments up to 8.3×.
  • Exact-well pairing. QC-adjusted within-treatment image-to-RNA correlation is 0.109 with real pairs versus −0.001 with broken pairs across 2,287 held-out wells (35/35 compound averages, 64/70 strata). Pooling 32 held-out wells, joint image/RNA exceeds RNA alone by +2.86 and +10.48 percentage points in the two batch directions; single-well classification shows no consistent gain.

Pillar 2 · Internal and external benchmarks

Better prediction on unseen chemistry and public drug data

Two internally generated benchmarking workstreams tested unseen recipes, unseen building blocks, active-learning screening compression, and transfer to public datasets (SciPlex3, NeurIPS 2023 primary human PBMCs, and TDC ADMET).

Investor lens

Why these benchmarks matter for capital efficiency

  • Predicting molecules before making them. Models predict the cellular effect of unmade combinations up to r = 0.58, and when a molecule uses a chemical part never seen in training, the additive recipe model edges the industry-standard graph neural network (Chemprop v2.3.1).
  • Fewer measurements. In retrospective replays across five of six objectives, model-directed selection matched the response recovery of 1,400 random measurements using 667–933 selections: 33–52% fewer. HSPA5 recovery reached 42.8% versus 30.7% frozen and 14.8% random. Prospective campaigns will test the laboratory savings.
  • Transfer to public data. A Z-Screen-pretrained model reaches DE50 response r = 0.593 on public SciPlex3 drugs, above released chemCPA (0.429) and scBalFlow (0.338). A training-duration control shows part of the gain over the standard scratch run comes from training length, so the defensible claim is the external ranking, not a fixed pretraining lift.
Technical lens

Quantitative summary and scope boundaries

  • Unseen building blocks (7,544 HEK293 compounds). Additive recipe model: program r = 0.363, R² = 0.109, 2.62× directional enrichment; Chemprop 2.3.1: 0.337, 0.089, 2.53×. Shared-context three-seed transformer: r = 0.398 and 2.86× using eight contexts, but program R² = −0.99 (ranking improves, calibration worsens).
  • SciPlex3 (83 held-out conditions, 9 drugs). Z-Screen-pretrained conditional Chemprop: all-gene R² 0.865 / Δr 0.476; DE50 R² 0.811 / Δr 0.593. Scratch trained to its validation optimum (58 epochs): 0.449 DE50 Δr; scratch stopped at the pretrained model’s 7 epochs: 0.618, with lower profile R² (0.794) and higher RMSE (0.184 versus 0.177).
  • PBMCs and ADMET. On 614 primary PBMC profiles (146 drugs × 6 cell types) the pinned k = 32 basis captures 17.59% of DE variance (32.29× random). Across 30 TDC runs (Caco-2, hERG, DILI), single-context HEK293 pretraining does not beat scratch Chemprop: the representations encode transcriptional biology, not ADMET physical chemistry.

Pillars 3 & 4 · Cross-atlas chemical and CRISPR phenomimicry

Chemical twins and genetic twins: turning uncharacterized chemistry into testable target programs

Z-Screen’s phenomimicry engine matches pilot-dataset profiles against 13,046 clinical and preclinical drugs (LINCS L1000, Tahoe-100M, CIGS) and 43 CRISPR Perturb-seq signature sets (~18,800 genes, ~8 million single cells), with calibration on reference controls at every step.

Investor lens

Facial recognition for cellular states

When a molecule enters a cell it changes thousands of genes, producing a high-dimensional barcode. We compare that barcode against two reference libraries:

  • Chemical twins. Series whose responses resemble established drugs provide an immediate mechanism hypothesis. Compound identifiers and response evidence are public; structures are withheld.
  • Genetic twins. When a disease target has no existing drug, matching against cells where a gene was silenced by CRISPR finds small-molecule twins for historically undruggable targets across the genome.
Technical lens

Calibrated scoring and bipartite mutual specificity

  • Three calibration choices for chemical references. Signed contrastive projections (unsigned-rank averaging would cancel a perfect match to 50%); vehicle-based centering against 72 pure DMSO wells rather than the 90.7%-active ZIC008 panel mean; and plate-level standardization of CIGS (replicate cosine 0.088 → 0.168). Together they give a 0.822 median matched-panel rank on 17 qualified controls (p = 0.0005) and zero-shot MZ1 chromatin-class AUROC 0.799 (ARV-825 ranked #2 of 1,940).
  • CRISPR scoring calibration. About 45% of genes in 25,000-gene Perturb-seq pseudobulks have MAD = 0, so z-scores use a positive MAD floor with a pure-vehicle baseline (+0.087 paired compound lift over an unfloored cosine, p = 0.045); the selected control-contrast bimodal ensemble reaches a 0.580 compound median (p = 0.043) on 25 matched Tier-1 compounds.
  • Selected target recoveries. STM2457 → METTL3 (#4 of 18,293), STC-15 → METTL3 (#1 of 2,393), BGT226 → PIK3CA (#1 of 18,293), AZD-7624 → MAPK14 (#15 of 2,620). These are selected atlas-specific ranks; the compound-level benchmark summarizes performance across the matched control set.

Pillar 5 · Prioritized portfolio and experimental triage

Twenty prioritized programs: ten chemical-twin series and ten genetic-twin programs

The two discovery tracks share chemistry: the BET-associated core BB_3717371053 appears in both. The chemical track has 110 qualifying reference matches across 51 series; the genetic track has 2,626 matches across 178 clusters. All ten genetic programs have at least three multi-well active analogs; chemical series ZS-SAR-01, -03 and -06 have none and ZS-SAR-07 has one, so those are rescreen-first. These are starting points for focused experiments, not validated leads.
